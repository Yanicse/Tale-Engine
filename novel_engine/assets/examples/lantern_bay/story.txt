The Salt Lantern

Chapter One: The Night the Light Went Out

Saltmere is a fishing town on the north coast of the Grey Reach, a long bay where fog rolls in from the sea almost every evening. The harbor is guarded by the Gull Tower, a white lighthouse older than the town charter. At the top of the tower burns the Salt Lantern, a great lens of green glass ground by a glassmaker whose name nobody remembers. Boats have steered by its light for two hundred years, and the town sells salt fish to half the coast because of it.

The keeper of the Gull Tower is Odrin Vale. He has tended the Salt Lantern for thirty-one years and has never missed a night. He is a thin, patient man with a cough that the winter damp makes worse, and he keeps the keys to the tower on a cord around his neck. He lives in two rooms at the foot of the stairs with a gray cat named Pilot.

Wren Ashby is seventeen and the apprentice of the ferrywoman Tamsin Reed. Every morning Wren rows the small ferry Dunlin across the harbor with the baker's boy, the schoolteacher and the early fishermen. Wren is quick with knots, bad at waking up, and curious about everything that happens on the water. Tamsin pays Wren four gulls a week, and Wren keeps the coins in a tin under the bed.

Saltmere counts its money in silver gulls and gold crowns. One gold crown is worth twenty silver gulls, and everyone in town knows the rate by heart, because the harbor fees are always asked in crowns and the wages are always paid in gulls.

The harbormaster, Corwen Pike, runs the docks with a ledger and a loud voice. He keeps the harbor fund in an iron chest in his office. The fund pays for the lantern's oil, the pier repairs and the winter wages of the pilots. Corwen is proud, and he has not told the town council that the chest has been almost empty since last spring.

On the evening of the Storm Festival, when the town lights paper boats and sets them on the water to thank the sea for the year, the Salt Lantern went dark. Odrin Vale was found at the foot of the stairs, dazed, with a bruise on his temple. The tower door was locked from the inside. The great lens was gone from its cradle, and only a ring of green glass dust was left on the iron floor.

Wren saw the light go out from the middle of the harbor, rowing the last ferry back from the festival. The fog was thick that night, and for a long minute the ferry drifted with no light at all. Wren steered by the sound of the harbor bell and reached the pier with shaking hands.

By midnight the whole town stood on the quay. Corwen Pike shouted for order and sent two watchmen to guard the tower. Tamsin Reed put a blanket around Wren and said only one thing: whoever took the lens must leave by water, because the only road out of Saltmere crosses the Marsh Bridge, and the bridge guard had seen nobody.

Chapter Two: The Inspector from Highmark

The next morning the packet boat from the capital, Highmark, brought an unexpected passenger. Inspector Lysa Brandt carried a leather case and a letter from the Crown Office. She had been sent to inspect the harbor accounts, and she arrived on the very day the lantern was stolen. Corwen Pike turned pale when he read her letter.

Lysa Brandt is a tall, calm woman who writes everything down in a small green notebook. She asked to see the tower first and the accounts second. She walked the stairs slowly, touched the empty cradle, and knelt to look at the glass dust on the floor. The dust, she said, was too fine to come from a broken lens. Someone had ground a piece of ordinary glass to make it look as if the lens had shattered.

Odrin Vale told the inspector what he remembered. He had climbed the stairs at dusk to light the lantern, as always. On the landing he heard a soft footstep behind him, and then nothing until he woke at the bottom of the stairs. He had smelled tar and cold smoke, he said, the smell of a man who works on the docks.

Wren had slipped into the tower behind the inspector, pretending to carry a message. On the third step Wren found a small brass token stamped with a wave and two crossed oars, caught in a crack of the stone. Wren put it in a pocket without a word and later showed it to Tamsin Reed, who went very quiet.

The token belonged to the Tidewrights, an old guild of boatbuilders and smugglers who work from the abandoned Cooper Yard at the east end of the harbor. Their leader is Maris Thorne, a broad, soft-spoken woman who repairs nets in daylight and trades in secrets by night. The Tidewrights have never stolen from the town, Tamsin said, because the town was where they hid their cargo. Something had changed.

That afternoon Wren overheard two dockhands in the ferry shed. One said that Maris Thorne had been seen buying a heavy wooden crate and a roll of oiled cloth. The other said that a buyer was waiting in Highmark and that the lens could be sold for five hundred gold crowns, enough to buy a small ship.

Chapter Three: Fog on the Reach

By the third evening the fog was so thick that the harbor bell rang every minute. The council had made a rule long ago: no boat may leave the harbor while the bell is ringing, because the sandbars of the Grey Reach are impossible to see in fog. The rule had saved many lives, and Corwen Pike enforced it strictly. It also meant that the thief, if the thief was still in town, could not sail away until the fog lifted.

Inspector Brandt asked Wren and Tamsin to help her. She wanted a ferry ready at the east pier without anyone noticing, and a guide who knew the water. Tamsin refused at first, because the Tidewrights were her neighbors and her old friends. Then she looked at the dark tower and agreed, on one condition: nobody would be hurt.

Corwen Pike, meanwhile, was watching the inspector. He had opened the harbor chest alone that morning and found, as he already knew, only seven silver gulls inside. He tried to persuade the council to end the inspection, and he offered to pay the inspector a fee of ten gold crowns to leave on the next packet. She wrote the offer in her notebook and said nothing.

At the Cooper Yard, Maris Thorne waited in the boat shed with the wooden crate on a trestle. Inside it, wrapped in oiled cloth, lay the Salt Lantern, whole and unbroken. She had not stolen it for money, she told the young visitor who found her there. Someone in the harbor office had paid her to take it, and had promised that the lantern would be returned after the festival, when the town would be too frightened to ask questions about the fund.

Wren listened to this in the dark and understood that the harbormaster had planned the theft to hide his empty chest, blaming the Tidewrights and drowning the missing money in the confusion. But the plan had gone wrong when Odrin Vale was hurt, and Maris Thorne no longer wished to give the lens back to the man who had hired her.

Out on the water the harbor bell kept ringing. Somewhere in the fog a boat was moving, with no light, toward the sandbars. Wren pulled the oars of the Dunlin hard, and the story of the Salt Lantern had only just begun.
